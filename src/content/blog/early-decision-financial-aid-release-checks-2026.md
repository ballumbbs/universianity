Most US early deadlines fall on **Sunday 1 November 2026**. Columbia, Harvard, Yale and MIT all close their early rounds that day, and so do most other colleges with early plans. Many families have one question left: *if we sign a binding Early Decision (ED) agreement and the aid isn't enough, are we stuck?*

The short answer is no, but the way out is narrower than many people think. This guide covers what the agreement actually says, when a financial-aid release usually applies, and the checks to do before anyone signs.

## ED, EA, REA and ED II at a glance

| Plan | Binding? | Can you apply early elsewhere? | Typical timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Early Decision (ED I)** | Yes | Non-binding Early Action is usually allowed, but only one ED | Apply by 1–2 Nov; results mid-December |
| **Early Action (EA)** | No | Usually yes | Apply by 1 Nov (varies); decide by 1 May |
| **Restrictive / Single-Choice EA (REA/SCEA)** | No | Restricted: generally no other private early applications | Apply by 1 Nov; results mid-December; decide by 1 May |
| **Early Decision II (ED II)** | Yes | Only one ED at a time | Apply roughly 1–15 Jan; results usually in February |

Harvard, Stanford, Caltech and Notre Dame use restrictive early action. Princeton and Yale use single-choice early action ([InGenius Prep's REA guide](https://ingeniusprep.com/blog/restrictive-early-action/)). Harvard's rule, for example, says REA applicants may not apply to any other private institution under an early decision, early action or restrictive early action plan, or to a binding early programme at a public university. Rules differ, so read each college's own wording.

As a guide to timing, last cycle's first early releases were Stanford on 12 December 2025 (4pm PT), Yale on 17 December (5pm ET) and Harvard no earlier than 18 December (7pm ET). This year's dates may differ; check [GradPilot's notification calendar](https://gradpilot.com/news/early-decision-early-action-notification-dates-2026-2027) and each college's page.

## What the ED agreement actually says

If you apply ED through the Common App, you sign an Early Decision Agreement. So do a parent or guardian and your school counsellor. The key clause, as reproduced by [GradPilot](https://gradpilot.com/news/early-decision-financial-aid-can-you-back-out) and in a copy posted by [Sewanee](https://new.sewanee.edu/files/resources/ed-agreement.pdf), reads:

> "If the student is accepted under an early decision plan, the student must promptly withdraw the applications submitted to other colleges and universities and make no additional applications to any other university in any country. If the student is an early decision candidate and is seeking financial aid, the student need not withdraw other applications until the student has received notification about financial aid from the admitting early decision institution."

The form you sign this year may be worded slightly differently. Read the version inside your own Common App account before you submit. Three details matter.

1. **"In any country" means what it says.** If you're admitted ED, you can't keep a UK, Canadian or other non-US application running as a backup. You also can't start new ones.
2. **The aid clause is about timing, not an automatic exit.** If you applied for aid, you can wait for the college's aid notification before withdrawing elsewhere. The clause doesn't promise a release.
3. **Colleges talk to each other.** You also acknowledge that the college may share your name and early commitment with other institutions. Quietly ignoring an ED admission is not a safe plan.

One more practical point: you can't withdraw a submitted application through the Common App website. If you're admitted ED, you must contact each other college directly ([how to do it](https://greatcollegeadvice.com/blog/how-to-withdraw-a-common-app-application/)).

## When can you be released for financial aid?

The agreement itself doesn't set a standard. Each college decides. Based on how the process usually works, according to admissions advisers such as [Spark Admissions](https://www.sparkadmissions.com/blog/can-you-rescind-an-early-decision-acceptance/) and [CollegeVine](https://blog.collegevine.com/is-early-decision-binding/):

- A release is generally for cases where the **final aid offer makes attending genuinely unaffordable**, not because another college might offer more.
- Before releasing you, colleges usually expect you to **ask for reconsideration or appeal** the offer first, often with documents.
- The process depends on **openness and direct contact** with the admissions and financial aid offices. You and your counsellor should communicate in writing.

These are typical practices described by consultancies, not official policy. Look for the exact release wording on your ED college's own page (for example, [Wesleyan](https://www.wesleyan.edu/admission/undergraduate-admission/early-decision.html) and [Washington and Lee](https://www.wlu.edu/admissions/apply/early-decision) explain their ED terms). If you can't find it, email the admissions office and ask: *What happens if our aid offer doesn't make attendance possible?* Keep the reply.

### How to ask for a release, step by step

1. **Read the aid offer carefully** as soon as it arrives. Separate grants (free money) from loans and work-study.
2. **Compare it with your own numbers**: what your family can actually pay each year, for four years.
3. **Request a review** from the financial aid office. Explain any changed or missing circumstances and attach documents.
4. **Involve your counsellor**, who also signed the agreement.
5. **If the revised offer still doesn't work, ask in writing to be released** from the ED commitment, and wait for written confirmation.
6. **Don't withdraw other applications, or ignore the ED offer, until the situation is settled**, within whatever reply deadline the college sets.

You may have read about legal challenges to early decision. One 2025 federal lawsuit has been reported, but it doesn't change the agreement you sign this year. Treat ED as fully binding.

## Checks to do before you sign (this week)

**1. Run the net price calculator.** Every US college has one on its website. Enter real figures, ideally with a parent, and save a screenshot. If the estimate is already beyond your budget, ED at that college is a gamble. Some families find ED aid is fair; [this nonprofit explainer](https://highambition.org/2025/02/26/dispelling-the-myth-the-truth-about-early-decision-and-financial-aid/) argues against the myth that ED always means less aid. But the calculator matters more than any general rule.

**2. Find the ED financial aid deadlines.** Aid forms (often the CSS Profile and/or FAFSA for US applicants, plus college-specific forms) frequently have early-round deadlines close to 1 November. Check the official aid page; if you miss them, you may get no aid estimate at all.

**3. If you're an international student, check need-blind vs need-aware.** Many colleges that are need-blind for US citizens are need-aware for international applicants. Some offer international students little or no need-based aid. Look this up on the college's own aid page, not a blog.

**4. Be realistic about your odds.** ED can help. In a compilation of 2025–26 Common Data Set figures ([source](https://vnis.edu.vn/en/united-states/early-decision-early-action-acceptance-rates/)), Dartmouth admitted 21.6% of ED applicants versus 3.8% of non-ED applicants. About 45% of all admits at Dartmouth and Barnard came through ED. But international applicants can face different odds: the same compilation shows Cornell admitting 3.6% of international applicants against 8.4% overall. Some colleges, including Duke, UChicago and Wake Forest, left their ED figures blank.

**5. Read the context.** Common App's 2025–26 data showed the slowest application growth (4%) at the most selective colleges and fewer international applications across all selectivity bands ([Common App update](https://www.commonapp.org/files/DAR/deadline-updates/2025-26/Common-App-Deadline-Update-2025.11.13.pdf)).

**6. Have the family conversation now.** Everyone who signs should agree on a firm maximum annual cost before you hit submit.

## ED II: a backup worth keeping

If you'd rather not commit by 1 November, ED II lets you make a binding choice in January, usually with results in February. ED II is just as binding as ED I. Official examples:

- **UChicago**: ED II deadline 4 January 2027 ([official page](https://collegeadmissions.uchicago.edu/apply/application/); check there for decision timing).
- **University of Miami**: ED II deadline 5 January ([official page](https://admissions.miami.edu/undergraduate/application-process/options-and-deadlines/index.html)).
- **Union College**: ED II deadline 15 January 2027 ([official calendar](https://www.union.edu/news/calendar/202701/early-decision-ii-application-deadline)).

ED II also works well after REA or SCEA. If you're deferred or denied in mid-December, you can generally apply ED II somewhere else ([Oriel's ED II list](https://orieladmissions.com/ed2-schools-list-2026/)). That route also gives you an extra six or so weeks to compare net price estimates.

## Quick FAQ

**Is Early Decision legally binding?**
It's a signed commitment rather than a typical contract enforced in court. Colleges treat it seriously, can share your commitment with other colleges, and may withdraw your offer if you break it. Treat it as binding.

**Can I get out of ED because another college offered more money?**
Generally no. A release is usually for aid that makes attendance impossible, not for comparing offers. That's what EA and REA are for.

**Can I apply ED and EA at the same time?**
Often yes, to non-binding EA colleges. But REA/SCEA colleges usually forbid it, and you can only have one ED. Check each college's rules.

**Do I have to withdraw my UK or other international applications if admitted ED?**
Yes. The agreement covers universities "in any country". You may wait until you receive the ED college's aid notification if you applied for aid.

**What if I miss the ED financial aid deadline?**
You risk getting no aid offer in December. Contact the aid office immediately and check their official policy.

## Next steps

Before 1 November, pick your top choice and spend one evening on it with a parent. Run its net price calculator with real numbers, find its early aid deadlines and international aid policy on the official site, and read the ED agreement inside your Common App account word for word. If the numbers work and everyone agrees on a cost ceiling, ED may be a strong move. If they don't, apply EA or REA now, keep ED II in January as your binding option, and make the decision with more information in hand.
