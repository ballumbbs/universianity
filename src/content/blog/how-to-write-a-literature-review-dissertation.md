If you're in your final year or on a taught master's, November is usually when the dissertation stops being an idea and turns into a pile of reading. For many students, the first chapter a supervisor asks to see is the **literature review**, often before the winter break.

This guide assumes you've never done a structured literature search. It covers the workflow most university guides share: search, evaluate, find themes, outline, then write. It also gives you two tools that make the job much easier, a worked Boolean search string and a synthesis matrix.

## What a literature review is (and isn't)

A literature review surveys the scholarly knowledge on your topic. A good one doesn't just summarise sources one after another. It **analyses, synthesises and critically evaluates** them to show where knowledge on the topic currently stands.

A few clarifications from university guides:

- **It isn't a book review.** [UC Santa Cruz](https://guides.library.ucsc.edu/write-a-literature-review) makes this distinction. You survey many sources, including scholarly articles, books, dissertations and conference proceedings, rather than judging a single one.
- **It's where your voice comes in.** The [University of Sheffield](https://sheffield.ac.uk/study-skills/writing/critical/literature-review) describes the review as a place for your "critical voice and interpretation."
- **It answers some basic questions.** [Brown University](https://libguides.brown.edu/organize/litreview) suggests asking: What research has been done on this topic? Who are the key researchers and experts in the field?

If a reader finishes your review knowing what's established, what's disputed and what's missing, and how your project fits into that, it has done its job.

## Narrative or systematic: which one are you writing?

Most undergraduate and master's dissertations include a **narrative literature review**. Some subjects, particularly in health, ask for a **systematic review**. Check your module handbook or ask your supervisor before you start, because the method is quite different.

| | Narrative review | Systematic review |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Broader, flexible | Narrow, tightly defined question |
| Search | Can be exploratory | Comprehensive, documented, reproducible |
| Typical use | Most dissertation chapters | Health sciences and evidence synthesis projects |
| Reporting standard | Your department's guidance | Usually PRISMA 2020 |
| Common criticism | Can lack synthesis and rigour | Time-consuming |

According to [McGill Library](https://mcgill.libanswers.com/health-sciences/faq/211884), narrative reviews are less comprehensive than systematic reviews and other forms of knowledge synthesis. A peer-reviewed article on [improving narrative reviews](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5803579/) notes that they can be broader in scope but have been criticised for lacking synthesis and rigour.

There is a useful middle ground. The [University of Waikato](https://libraryguides.waikato.ac.nz/literaturereviews/narrative) notes that narrative reviews using **systematic methods**, meaning structured searching and selection, are becoming more popular. For a dissertation, this is often the best option. You keep the flexibility of a narrative review, and you can still explain exactly how you found your sources.

## Step 1: Search the literature properly

### Build a keyword list

Start with your research question and split it into two or three **core concepts**. For each concept, list synonyms, related terms, alternative spellings and the technical terms used in your field. Keep adding to the list as you read, because good papers will show you vocabulary you didn't know to search for.

### Use Boolean operators

The [University of Toronto](https://guides.library.utoronto.ca/c.php?g=577919&p=3987297) describes Boolean operators as what turns your research question into a query a database can understand. The rule of thumb:

- **OR broadens** your search. Use it to link synonyms *within* one concept.
- **AND narrows** your search. Use it to combine *different* concepts.

### A worked example

Say your question is: *How does remote working affect employee wellbeing?*

1. **Concept 1, remote work:** remote work, working from home, telework, hybrid work
2. **Concept 2, wellbeing:** wellbeing, well-being, burnout, stress

Combine the synonyms with OR, put brackets around each group, then join the groups with AND:

`("remote work" OR "working from home" OR telework OR "hybrid work") AND (wellbeing OR "well-being" OR burnout OR stress)`

The quotation marks keep multi-word phrases together in most databases. Syntax varies between platforms, so check each database's help page. Once you have results, use the database filters (date range, document type, subject area) to narrow them further. The [UNT Health Science Center guide](https://libguides.unthsc.edu/c.php?g=835192&p=9739239) shows what these look like in Web of Science.

For more depth, [McMaster's Health Sciences Library](https://hslmcmaster.libguides.com/srm/boolean) sets out an eight-step approach that runs from identifying your topic to managing your search strategy and results. [London South Bank University](https://library.lsbu.ac.uk/literaturesearching/boolean) has a clear UK-based explainer.

**Tip:** Record every search string you use, which database you used it in, the date and the number of results. You'll need this if you write a methods-style paragraph, and it saves you from repeating searches.

## Step 2: Evaluate your sources

Not everything you find deserves a place in the review. UC Santa Cruz describes this stage as deciding which literature makes a **significant contribution** to understanding your topic. For each source, ask:

- Is it peer-reviewed, or otherwise scholarly?
- Is it directly relevant to my question, or only loosely related?
- Is the method sound for what it claims?
- Is it current enough? Or is it a foundational study that everyone else cites?
- Who wrote it, and do the same names keep coming up? (This is Brown's "key researchers" question.)

## Step 3: Find themes, debates and gaps with a synthesis matrix

This is the most useful tool in the whole process. A **synthesis matrix** (Sheffield calls it a "Literature Matrix") is a spreadsheet with **one row per source** and **columns for themes, methods and findings**. Reading across a row tells you what one source says. Reading **down a column** tells you what the whole literature says about one issue, and that column view is what synthesis means in practice.

Here's a simplified example for the remote-work question. The sources are placeholders; use your own.

| Source | Method | Theme: isolation | Theme: flexibility | Theme: burnout | Notes / gaps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Author A (year) | Survey | Reports increased isolation | — | Links to long hours | UK sample only |
| Author B (year) | Interviews | Mixed | Strong positive | — | Small sample |
| Author C (year) | Longitudinal | — | Positive at first, fades | Rises over time | Pre-2020 data |

Patterns start to show quickly. Where do sources agree? Where do they contradict each other? Which methods are overused, and which populations are missing? Those gaps are often where your own project fits.

You don't have to build the matrix from scratch. There are free templates:

- [Central Michigan University](https://libguides.cmich.edu/lit_review/LR_synthesize) offers downloadable Word and Excel versions.
- [National University](https://resources.nu.edu/researchtools/synthesisandanalysis) has a template and handout, and explains the difference between synthesis and analysis.
- [FIU Writing Center](https://case.fiu.edu/writingcenter/online-resources/_assets/synthesis-matrix-2.pdf) has a short PDF handout.

Brown also suggests a **concept map**: a sketch of how your themes connect. It works well alongside the matrix if you think visually.

## Step 4: Outline the structure

The [University of Kent](https://student.kent.ac.uk/studies/literature-reviews/writing-a-literature-review) advises starting to write only once you've done the reading, note-taking and planning. Your matrix columns usually become your sections.

Common ways to organise a review:

- **Thematic:** one section per theme (isolation, flexibility, burnout). This is the most common approach and usually the strongest.
- **By perspective or discipline:** for example, grouping sources into sociological, historical and cultural approaches, as [Scribbr](https://www.scribbr.com/dissertation/literature-review/) illustrates.
- **Methodological:** comparing what different research methods have found.

A basic shape that works for most dissertations:

1. **Introduction:** set out the focus and purpose of the review and how you've organised it.
2. **Body sections:** one per theme, each combining several sources.
3. **Gaps and debates:** what's unresolved or under-researched.
4. **Conclusion:** summarise the state of knowledge and link it clearly to your research question.

[Loma Linda University](https://libguides.llu.edu/literaturereview/writing) says a review generally has five components and should show "signs of synthesis and understanding." If you're writing a shorter review inside a research paper, [UC San Diego Psychology](https://psychology.ucsd.edu/undergraduate-program/undergraduate-resources/academic-writing-resources/writing-research-papers/writing-lit-review.html) has an "express method" worth reading.

On length, there's no universal rule. Check your module handbook or ask your supervisor.

## Step 5: Write with synthesis, not summary

The most common mistake is writing paragraphs that each describe one source: *"Smith (2020) found... Jones (2021) found..."* Lead with the **idea**, and use the sources as evidence for it:

- **Summary:** "Author A found remote workers felt isolated. Author B found they valued flexibility."
- **Synthesis:** "Studies broadly agree that remote work increases flexibility (Author B; Author C), but evidence on isolation is mixed, and most of it comes from small or single-country samples (Author A; Author B)."

The second version shows your critical voice. It compares sources, weighs the evidence and points to limitations.

## If you're doing a systematic review: PRISMA 2020

If your department requires a systematic review, you'll probably need to follow **PRISMA 2020**. The [PRISMA 2020 statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33782057/) provides a 27-item checklist, a checklist for abstracts and revised flow diagrams. The **flow diagram** shows how many records you identified, how many you included and excluded, and why you excluded them. You can download templates from the [official PRISMA site](https://www.prisma-statement.org/prisma-2020-flow-diagram), and [UNC Chapel Hill's guide](https://guides.lib.unc.edu/prisma) explains how to build one.

## A quick caution on AI tools

Discovery tools such as Semantic Scholar (run by the Allen Institute for AI and publicly released in November 2015), and others listed in commercial roundups, can help you find papers. Treat them as a starting point only. **Open, read and check every source yourself** before you cite it, and never cite a paper you haven't seen. Your university's rules on AI use and how to declare it vary, so see our article "Using AI for Coursework in 2026" and your module handbook.

## Checklist before you send the draft to your supervisor

- [ ] My introduction states the focus, purpose and structure of the review.
- [ ] Sections are organised by theme or argument, not source by source.
- [ ] Most paragraphs cite more than one source.
- [ ] I've identified agreements, debates and gaps.
- [ ] The conclusion links clearly to my research question.
- [ ] I've recorded my search strings, databases and dates.
- [ ] I've read every source I cite, and all references are correctly formatted.
- [ ] (Systematic reviews) I've completed the PRISMA flow diagram and checklist.

## Quick FAQ

**How many sources do I need?**
There's no fixed number. It depends on your subject, level and word count. Ask your supervisor what they expect, and aim to cover the key researchers and the main debates.

**Can I write the literature review before collecting my data?**
Usually, yes. Many students draft it first. Expect to revise it later, because your findings may make some sources more or less relevant.

**What's the difference between a narrative review and a systematic review?**
A narrative review is broader and more flexible. A systematic review uses a comprehensive, documented search and normally follows PRISMA 2020. Most dissertations use a narrative review, ideally with structured searching.

**Do I have to use a synthesis matrix?**
No, but it's the easiest way to stop yourself summarising sources one by one, and the free templates take minutes to set up.

## Next steps

This week, write your research question in one sentence, split it into two or three concepts and draft your first Boolean search string. Run it in one or two databases your library subscribes to, and log the results. Then download a synthesis matrix template, add your ten most relevant sources and look down the columns for themes. Once those themes are clear, outline your sections and book a meeting with your supervisor before you start writing the full draft.
