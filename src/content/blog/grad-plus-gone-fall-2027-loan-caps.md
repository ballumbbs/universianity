If you're applying to a US master's, PhD or professional programme that starts in fall 2027, you'll be part of the first full intake to apply entirely under the new federal loan rules. Most graduate deadlines fall between December and January, so now is the time to budget for them.

The headline change is that **the Graduate PLUS Loan no longer exists for new borrowers.** Before July 1, 2026, graduate and professional students could borrow up to their full cost of attendance through Grad PLUS. Now there's a hard ceiling, and many students will face a gap between what federal loans cover and what a programme costs.

This guide is for US citizens and eligible non-citizens who qualify for federal student aid. International students can't use these loans anyway, but the section on covering the gap still applies to you.

## What changed, in one paragraph

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed on July 4, 2025, ended Grad PLUS for students starting a new graduate or professional programme after July 1, 2026. New students can now borrow only **Federal Direct Unsubsidized Loans**, which come with fixed annual and lifetime limits. [Credible](https://www.credible.com/student-loans/student-loan-changes) notes that Grad PLUS is being phased out completely by 2028, once the students covered by the transition rules have finished.

## The new borrowing limits

| | Before July 1, 2026 | New students from July 1, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Grad PLUS | Up to full cost of attendance | Not available |
| Graduate students (annual) | Unsubsidized loan plus Grad PLUS | $20,500 |
| Graduate students (aggregate) | Effectively no cap via Grad PLUS | $100,000 |
| Professional students (annual) | Unsubsidized loan plus Grad PLUS | $50,000 |
| Professional students (aggregate) | Effectively no cap via Grad PLUS | $200,000 |
| Lifetime cap (all study) | n/a | $257,500 |

A few details matter more than they first appear:

- **Most master's degrees, including the MBA, use the graduate limits.** That means $20,500 a year and $100,000 in total ([ETS overview](https://www.ets.org/grad-school-journey/student-loan-changes-2026.html)).
- **Undergraduate borrowing doesn't count towards the graduate or professional caps.** The Education Department confirmed this in its [official notice to schools](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/knowledge-center/library/electronic-announcements/2026-04-24/one-big-beautiful-bill-act-nslds-eligibility-processing-updates).
- **It does count towards the $257,500 lifetime cap.** That cap covers undergraduate, graduate and professional borrowing, regardless of whether loans were repaid, forgiven or discharged. Parent PLUS loans don't count towards it.
- **The caps don't reset when you start a new degree.** If you borrow for a master's and then a second master's, both draw from the same $100,000. The combined graduate-plus-professional total is $200,000 ([Washington State University](https://financialaid.wsu.edu/2025/11/10/one-big-beautiful-bill-act-2026-27-changes-to-federal-financial-aid/)).

## Are you a "graduate" or a "professional" student?

This one question decides whether your annual limit is $20,500 or $50,000, so it's worth getting right.

The Education Department's RISE (Reimagining and Improving Student Education) final rule, published on May 1, 2026, limited the programmes counted as "professional" for borrowing purposes to 11 fields. Law and medicine are the classic examples. Programmes outside that list are treated as graduate programmes and get the lower limits.

The definition has been fiercely contested. Several states have [sued over the limits on certain nursing and healthcare degrees](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/19/nx-s1-5826688/lawsuit-student-loans-nursing-healthcare-graduate-degree), and fields such as nursing and social work have been at the centre of the fight. Classifications may change while those disputes play out.

**What to do:** don't assume your programme counts as professional. Email the financial aid office of every school you're applying to. Ask which annual and aggregate limit applies to your specific programme for 2027–28, and keep their written reply.

## Studying part-time? You'll get less

The annual limits assume full-time enrolment. If you take fewer credits, your loan is reduced in proportion to your course load.

- At the [University of Washington](https://www.washington.edu/financialaid/2026/06/05/federal-loan-updates-for-2026-27/), full time means 10 credits for graduate students and 12 for professional students. Each school sets its own thresholds.
- [Johns Hopkins](https://seam.jhu.edu/financial-aid/financial-aid-faq/one-big-beautiful-bill-act-faqs/) gives this worked example: a student taking 9 credits in the fall would get 50% (9/18) of the $20,500 annual limit, or $10,250, for that term.

If you're planning to work while studying part-time, ask each school how it will calculate your reduced loan.

## Already a grad student? The legacy provision

Some current students can keep borrowing under the old Grad PLUS rules for up to three more academic years, or until they finish their programme if that comes sooner. To qualify, you must:

1. have been enrolled in a programme of study at an institution as of June 30, 2026, **and**
2. have received a Direct Loan for that programme before July 1, 2026, **and**
3. stay continuously enrolled in that same programme.

**Warning for anyone moving from a master's to a PhD:** switching to a new programme means losing this protection. Your new programme will fall under the new caps, and any graduate borrowing you've already done counts towards the $100,000 aggregate. [NYU](https://www.nyu.edu/admissions/financial-aid-and-scholarships/revisions-to-federal-student-loans-in-the-one-big-beautiful-bill.html) and [Augusta University](https://www.augusta.edu/finaid/obbba.php) both explain the conditions in detail.

## Repayment works differently now too

Loans made on or after July 1, 2026, come with a new set of repayment plans:

- **Two new plans launched on July 1, 2026:** the Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) and the Tiered Standard Plan.
- **RAP is the only income-driven repayment plan for new loans.** On an income-driven plan, your monthly payment depends on what you earn.
- **New loans can affect old ones.** If you already have federal loans and you take out new ones or consolidate, you may be limited to RAP for your *entire* balance, not just the new loans ([Student Loan Planner](https://www.studentloanplanner.com/repayment-assistance-plan-rap/)).

Sources currently disagree on which plan new borrowers are placed in by default if they don't choose one. Check the official Federal Student Aid site, studentaid.gov, before you sign anything, and choose your plan actively rather than relying on a default.

## How to cover the funding gap: step by step

### 1. Work out your real gap

For each offer, use this formula:

**Cost of attendance − federal loan limit − grants and scholarships = your gap**

Here's a hypothetical example. A master's programme with a $70,000 cost of attendance and a $15,000 scholarship leaves a gap of $70,000 − $20,500 − $15,000 = **$34,500 a year** that Grad PLUS would once have covered. Use each school's official cost of attendance figure. It includes living costs, not just tuition.

### 2. Hunt for assistantships, fellowships and tuition waivers

Ask each department directly about teaching or research assistantships, fellowships and tuition waivers. These are especially common in PhD programmes and can wipe out a gap entirely. Ask what's available after the first year too, not just at admission.

### 3. Check employer tuition assistance

If you're working now, ask HR whether your employer helps pay for graduate study and what strings are attached, such as staying for a set period afterwards.

### 4. Treat private loans with caution

Private loans can fill a gap, but they don't come with federal protections such as RAP or Public Service Loan Forgiveness. Compare interest rates, fees, co-signer requirements and hardship options carefully, and borrow only what you need.

### 5. Let the gap shape your decision

A cheaper offer with a smaller gap can beat a more prestigious one that leaves you with heavy private debt. Once you have offers, it's often possible to appeal a financial aid package, especially if a comparable school has offered more. Ask politely, and bring the numbers.

For more ideas, [Pace University](https://www.pace.edu/news/graduate-school-funding-changes) has a recent rundown of ways new grad students are covering the shortfall.

## Sidebar: Parent PLUS has limits now too

If you have a younger sibling heading to college, parents of dependent undergraduates who use Parent PLUS loans now face limits of $20,000 a year and $65,000 in total per student ([SCI-Arc comparison table](https://www.sciarc.edu/admissions/financial-aid/federal-student-aid/changes-to-federal-student-loans-2026)).

## Quick FAQ

**Can I still get a Grad PLUS loan for fall 2027?**
Not if you're starting a new graduate or professional programme. Only students covered by the legacy provision can keep using it, and only within their existing programme.

**Does my undergraduate debt reduce my $100,000 graduate limit?**
No. It doesn't count towards the graduate or professional caps, but it does count towards the $257,500 lifetime cap.

**Is an MBA a professional degree for loan purposes?**
No. Most master's degrees, including the MBA, use the graduate limits of $20,500 a year and $100,000 in total.

**My programme might be reclassified. What should I do?**
Ask the school's financial aid office in writing which limit applies, and budget for the lower figure until the classification is confirmed.

**I'm an international student. Does any of this apply to me?**
The federal loans don't, but the strategies for covering costs still do: assistantships, fellowships, waivers and comparing offers.

## Next steps

This week, list every programme you're applying to and email each financial aid office three questions: Is my programme classed as graduate or professional? What full-time credit load do you require for the full loan? What assistantships, fellowships or waivers can I apply for, and by when? Then build a simple spreadsheet showing each school's cost of attendance, your likely loan limit and the gap left over. When offers arrive, you'll be comparing real numbers, not sticker prices. Rules in this area are still shifting, so confirm the details on studentaid.gov and your school's official pages before you commit.
