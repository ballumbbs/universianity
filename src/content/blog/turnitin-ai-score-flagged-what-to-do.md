It's November, coursework marks are coming back, and some students are opening feedback that mentions an "AI writing" score. If that's you, or you're worried it soon will be, this guide explains what the number actually means, what it doesn't mean, and what to do next.

One thing first: **an AI score is not a verdict.** Turnitin says itself that it does not decide whether misconduct happened. That judgement belongs to your instructor and your university ([Turnitin on false positives](https://www.turnitin.com/blog/understanding-false-positives-within-our-ai-writing-detection-capabilities)).

## How the Turnitin AI score works

### It's not your similarity score

Turnitin produces two different things:

- **Similarity score:** how much of your text matches existing sources, such as websites, journals and other students' papers.
- **AI writing score:** an estimate of how much of your text looks like it was produced by a generative AI tool.

These measure completely different things. A high similarity score with a 0% AI score is common, and so is the reverse. Turnitin's own guidance warns that the AI percentage "may not always be accurate" ([BUE instructor guide](https://bue.libguides.com/c.php?g=144329&p=9698569)). Turnitin also describes the report as not absolute proof ([Turnitin for academic leaders](https://www.turnitin.com/blog/ai-writing-detection-what-academic-leaders-need-to-know-as-technology-matures)).

### What the asterisk (*%) means

Scores between 1% and 19% are no longer shown as a number. They appear as an asterisk instead, because false positives are more common in that low range ([University of Melbourne staff guide](https://lms.unimelb.edu.au/staff/guides/canvas/assignments/turnitin-accessing-and-interpreting-the-ai-writing-report)).

| What appears | What it means | What you should do |
|---|---|---|
| **\*%** | Some AI-like text was detected, but at low confidence (1–19%) | Not an accusation and not a clean pass. Keep your drafts, just in case |
| **A number, 20% or higher** | Turnitin's model is more confident that parts of the text look AI-generated | Expect your instructor to look closer. Gather your evidence now |
| **No AI score at all** | Your university may have switched the detector off | Check your institution's AI policy, because other checks may still apply |

Some universities don't use the detector at all. Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's AI detector in August 2023, citing concerns about false positives and the lack of advance notice before it launched ([Vanderbilt's explanation](https://www.vanderbilt.edu/brightspace/2023/08/16/guidance-on-ai-detection-and-why-were-disabling-turnitins-ai-detector/)).

## How accurate is it, really?

Turnitin says its document-level false positive rate is under 1%. That figure applies under specific conditions, so check [Turnitin's own explanation](https://www.turnitin.com/blog/understanding-false-positives-within-our-ai-writing-detection-capabilities) for the details. Turnitin has also said its detector misses roughly 15% of AI-generated text ([University of San Diego guide](https://lawlibguides.sandiego.edu/c.php?g=1443311&p=10721367); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_content_detection)).

Accuracy also differs between whole documents and individual sentences. A single highlighted sentence is weaker evidence than a whole paper flagged ([Turnitin on sentence-level false positives](https://www.turnitin.com/blog/understanding-the-false-positive-rate-for-sentences-of-our-ai-writing-detection-capability)).

Even a low error rate adds up across millions of submissions, so some honest students do get flagged. Writing that is highly polished, very formal, heavily structured or written in a second language can look more "AI-like" to detectors. If English isn't your first language, the habits in this guide matter even more for you.

## What changed in 2025–26

### Humaniser tools are now a target

On 27 August 2025, Turnitin launched **AI bypasser detection** ([press release](https://www.turnitin.com/press/turnitin-expands-capabilities-amid-rising-threats-posed-by-ai-bypassers)). It flags text that was generated by AI and then run through a "humaniser" or paraphrasing tool to disguise it. It's available to institutions with the Turnitin Originality add-on and to iThenticate 2.0 customers. Turnitin has since updated this detection further ([IT Brief](https://itbrief.com.au/story/turnitin-updates-tool-to-detect-ai-bypassers-humanised-text)). You can read Turnitin's reasoning in its [bypasser explainer](https://www.turnitin.com/blog/navigating-ai-misuse-tackling-the-impact-of-ai-bypassers-on-academic-integrity).

Turnitin's model page also notes that updates are **not retroactive**. An older submission only gets scored by a newer model if it is resubmitted ([AI writing detection model](https://guides.turnitin.com/hc/en-us/articles/28294949544717-AI-writing-detection-model)). Model versions change during the year, so check that page and the [release notes](https://guides.turnitin.com/hc/en-us/articles/27251688507533-Turnitin-release-notes) for the current version.

### Turnitin Clarity: showing your process

Some courses now use **Turnitin Clarity**. It's a writing space that records how you write, and it allows AI use within limits your instructor sets. Turnitin published a [student guide](https://www.turnitin.com/papers/writing-with-turnitin-clarity-a-guide-for-students) in May 2026 and [early findings](https://www.turnitin.com/press/turnitin-data-shows-transparency-about-ai-use-benefits-students-and-educators) in February 2026.

In Australia and New Zealand, students who used Clarity were about 40% less likely to submit work flagged as mostly AI-generated ([IT Brief, Oct 2026](https://itbrief.com.au/story/turnitin-says-clarity-cuts-ai-written-student-work)). According to the release notes, Clarity became mobile-friendly on 9 September 2026, and instructors gained customisable assignment templates on 28 October 2026.

If your module offers Clarity, consider using it. A recorded writing process is strong protection against a false flag.

## Flagged? Do these things, in this order

This is general guidance, not legal advice. Your university's own procedure always comes first.

1. **Stay calm and don't delete anything.** Keep every draft, outline, set of notes, reading list, browser history and message with classmates or tutors about the work. Deleting files, even messy ones, can look bad later.
2. **Export your version history.** Google Docs and Microsoft 365 keep a timestamped record of how a document changed. A history that shows the essay growing over days, with false starts and edits, is some of the most persuasive evidence you can have. Version history isn't perfect proof, and your university can't automatically access your files, so you'll need to share it yourself ([Vappingo](https://www.vappingo.com/word-blog/can-universities-detect-ai-use/)).
3. **Ask for the evidence in writing.** Politely request:
   - which tool flagged your work, and which model version
   - the exact score
   - the highlighted passages
   - which policy you're alleged to have breached
4. **Read your university's rules.** Find the academic misconduct procedure and the AI policy for your course or module. Note any deadlines for responding.
5. **Get free, independent help.** In the UK and Australia, your students' union or student guild advice service can usually support you and may come to meetings with you. In the US, students at public universities have due-process rights, and if a case escalates seriously, some students seek a lawyer ([Nesenoff & Miltenberg](https://nmllplaw.com/blog/when-ai-gets-you-accused-what-to-do-if-your-school-says-you-used-chatgpt/)).
6. **Prepare to talk about your work.** Be ready to explain your argument, your sources and why you made particular choices. If you wrote it, a conversation or viva is often the fastest way to show it.
7. **Use the formal appeal route if needed.** If the outcome seems wrong, follow your university's appeal process. UK students who finish internal procedures may be able to go to an independent body such as the Office of the Independent Adjudicator (England and Wales). Check eligibility on its official site.

It can work. Two UC Davis students were cleared in 2023, one of them partly by showing Google Docs version history ([GradPilot](https://gradpilot.com/news/falsely-accused-ai-cheating-what-to-do)).

### What not to do

- **Don't run your work through a humaniser tool**, either before or after a flag. Turnitin specifically targets these tools now, and using one can look like deliberate concealment.
- **Don't rely on free online "AI checkers" as proof.** Different detectors disagree, and a clean result from one won't settle a case.
- **Don't send an angry email.** Keep everything factual, polite and written down.

## Prevention: habits that protect you

- **Write in Google Docs or Word with version history on.** Start in the same document instead of pasting in a finished draft from somewhere else.
- **Keep your notes and outlines**, including handwritten ones, until marks are final.
- **Declare and cite any permitted AI use.** If your module allows AI for brainstorming or feedback, say so in the format your course asks for. Our earlier guide, *Using AI for Coursework in 2026*, covers what's usually allowed and how to cite it.
- **Use Clarity if it's offered.** It records your process for you.
- **Know your own work.** If you can explain every paragraph, you're in a strong position.

## Quick FAQ

**Does an asterisk (*%) mean I'm in trouble?**
No. It means the score was between 1% and 19%, a range where Turnitin treats results as low confidence. It isn't an accusation.

**Can Turnitin's AI detector be wrong?**
Yes. Turnitin acknowledges false positives and says it misses around 15% of AI text. It also says the report is not proof and that humans must make the decision.

**Will my old essay be rescored by a newer model?**
Not automatically. Turnitin says model updates aren't retroactive, so only resubmitted work gets the new score.

**Do humaniser or paraphrasing tools get around detection?**
Turnitin has detected AI bypassers since August 2025 and has updated that feature since. Using these tools also risks a misconduct finding on its own.

**What's the single best piece of evidence if I'm falsely flagged?**
A detailed, timestamped version history, backed up by your notes and your ability to discuss the work confidently.
