Most students study by re-reading notes and highlighting. It *feels* productive, but it mostly builds familiarity, not memory. Two techniques consistently do better: **active recall** (testing yourself) and **spaced repetition** (reviewing at growing intervals).

## What is active recall?

Active recall means pulling information *out* of your memory instead of putting it back *in*. Every time you successfully retrieve something, the memory gets stronger.

Ways to practise it:

1. **Close the book and write** everything you remember about a topic, then check.
2. **Turn headings into questions.** "Causes of the French Revolution" becomes "What were the three main causes of the French Revolution?"
3. **Do past papers** before you feel ready — mistakes show you exactly what to review.
4. **Teach it** to a friend (or an empty chair) in plain language.

## What is spaced repetition?

You forget quickly after learning something, then more slowly each time you review it. Spaced repetition takes advantage of this by reviewing just before you would forget.

| Review | When |
| --- | --- |
| 1st | Same day you learn it |
| 2nd | 1–2 days later |
| 3rd | About a week later |
| 4th | About a month later |

If you get something wrong, bring it back to a short interval.

## A simple weekly system

1. **After each class:** write 5–10 questions on the key ideas.
2. **Each evening (20 min):** answer today's questions and anything due for review.
3. **Weekend (45 min):** do one timed past paper or problem set, and log every mistake as a new question.
4. **Before exams:** focus only on the questions you still get wrong.

Flashcard apps can schedule reviews for you, but a notebook with dated columns works too.

## Common mistakes

- Making beautiful flashcards and never testing yourself with them.
- Reviewing only what you already know because it feels good.
- Cramming everything into the night before — spacing beats volume.

## Quick FAQ

**How long until I see results?** Most students notice better recall within two to three weeks of consistent practice.

**Does this work for maths and science?** Yes — use practice problems as your "questions", not just definitions.

**Is re-reading ever useful?** A quick skim to get an overview is fine; just follow it with self-testing.

## Next steps

Pick one subject this week. After your next class, write ten questions, answer them that evening, and schedule reviews for two days and one week later. Small, consistent sessions beat marathon cramming.
