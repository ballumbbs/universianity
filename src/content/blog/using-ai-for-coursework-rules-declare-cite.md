It's late October, and the first assessed essays of the year are due in November and December. If you're using AI to help, you're far from alone. Using it is not usually the problem. Using it in a way your assignment doesn't allow, or failing to say that you used it, can turn a good essay into an academic misconduct case.

This guide covers how to find out what's allowed, what's usually fine and what isn't, how to declare AI use, how to cite it, and how to protect yourself with evidence.

## Why the rules feel so confusing

Almost everyone is using AI now. The [HEPI/Kortext Student Generative AI Survey 2026](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2026/) (HEPI Report 199, published 12 March 2026) surveyed 1,054 full-time UK undergraduates:

- About **95%** use AI in at least one way.
- **94%** say they use generative AI to help with assessed work.
- **49%** think AI has improved their student experience, mostly by saving time, improving understanding and giving instant support.

The [2025 survey](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2025/) put AI use at 92%, so it's still rising. HEPI also found that universities' own adoption is lagging behind student behaviour. In practice, students are often moving faster than the guidance.

There's also no single rule. The [Russell Group principles on generative AI](https://www.russellgroup.ac.uk/policy/policy-briefings/principles-use-generative-ai-tools-education) say that "the appropriate uses of generative AI tools are likely to differ between academic disciplines". They also commit universities to ensuring that "academic rigour and integrity is upheld". In other words, the rules depend on your subject, and they will be enforced.

Some examples of how much the rules vary:

| Institution | Default approach (check the official page) |
|---|---|
| **Oxford** | AI can be used for personal study and skills development. Using it in assessed work needs explicit authorisation. Oxford has offered free ChatGPT Edu to all students and staff since the 2025–26 academic year. |
| **Imperial** | Students are told to check their department's policy on using and disclosing AI. There isn't one rule for every assessment. |
| **Dundee** | Where AI is allowed, the assessment brief should say whether you must disclose it and how. |
| **Sydney / Toronto / MIT** | According to [secondary policy roundups](https://textpulse.ai/blog/university-ai-policy), Sydney allows AI in open assessments unless told otherwise, Toronto bars it by default, and MIT leaves it to each instructor. Confirm on the universities' own sites. |

The main lesson is simple. **If AI use hasn't been clearly authorised for an assessment, don't assume it's allowed.** Oxford's [student FAQ](https://www.ox.ac.uk/students/life/it/genai-tools/genai-faqs-for-students) puts it this way: use of GenAI "should always be in consultation with course providers".

## The three-step check before you use AI on any assignment

Do this for every assessment, even if the last one allowed AI.

1. **Read the assessment brief.** This is the most specific rule and usually takes priority. Look for words like "AI", "generative tools", "declaration" or "disclosure".
2. **Check the module handbook.** It may set a default for the whole module, such as "AI permitted for planning only".
3. **Check your department or school policy.** This fills any gaps and usually explains how to declare AI use.
4. **Still unclear? Ask in writing.** Email your module leader or tutor with a specific question, for example: "Can I use ChatGPT to check the structure of my essay plan, and do I need to declare it?" Keep the reply.

## Usually fine vs usually misconduct

Your brief always takes priority, but this is a useful starting point.

**Often acceptable for learning (where AI isn't banned):**

- Getting a difficult concept explained in simpler language
- Quizzing yourself on lecture content before an exam
- Brainstorming angles or making a revision timetable
- Getting feedback on whether your own plan makes sense

**Usually misconduct:**

- Submitting AI-generated text as your own writing
- Using AI on an assessment where it's prohibited
- Using AI but not declaring it when declaration is required
- Citing sources an AI suggested without checking they exist and say what it claims

That last point matters. Dundee's [core policy on generative AI](https://www.dundee.ac.uk/corporate-information/core-policy-generative-ai-education-and-student-experience) says students must not assume AI output is "accurate, unbiased, lawful, ethical, properly referenced, or appropriate for academic use". You are responsible for everything you submit.

## How to declare AI use

HEPI's [Policy Note 71](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/What-UK-university-AI-policies-actually-do-A-study-of-96-institutions.pdf) (May 2026) looked at the AI policies of 96 UK institutions. The most common model is **conditional trust**: you're trusted to use AI appropriately, as long as you declare it and keep evidence of your process.

Your university may have its own form or cover sheet. If so, use it. If you just need a written statement, this template covers what most policies ask for:

> **AI use statement:** I used [tool and model, e.g. ChatGPT] on [dates] to [specific purpose, e.g. explain the difference between two theories and suggest a structure for my essay plan]. I did not use it to [e.g. write any of the submitted text]. I checked its output against [sources/lecture notes] and made the final decisions on content and wording myself. Chat links: [shareable URLs].

Be specific. "I used AI a bit" doesn't tell the marker anything. "I used it to generate practice questions on week 3's reading" does.

**Accessibility note:** some policies treat AI used as a reasonable adjustment differently. At Dundee, students using AI for adjustments recommended by Disability Services don't need to declare it, unless their use goes beyond that adjustment. If this applies to you, check with your own disability or wellbeing service.

## How to cite AI in APA (2025 update)

APA updated its guidance in September 2025. Most AI chat tools now let you share a conversation with a unique URL and title, so APA now recommends citing the **specific chat** rather than only the tool. See the [official APA Style guidance](https://apastyle.apa.org/blog/cite-generative-ai-references) for full details.

### Citing a specific chat

Format:

`AI Company. (Year, Month Day). Title of chat [Generative AI chat]. AI Tool/Model. Shareable URL`

Example:

OpenAI. (2026, September 23). *Explanation of AI bias* [Generative AI chat]. ChatGPT. [shareable URL]

In-text: **(OpenAI, 2026)**

### Citing the tool in general

`AI Company. (Year). AI Tool [Large language model]. URL`

### Things to remember

- **No version numbers needed.** Use "ChatGPT" or a specific model name such as "ChatGPT-5", but you don't need the version number.
- **Go back to the original source.** If AI points you to a book, paper or report, open it, read it and cite *that*, not the AI summary.
- **Describe substantial use.** If AI contributed significantly, explain how you used it in your methods section, acknowledgements, an appendix or wherever your brief says.

**Using MLA, Harvard or another style?** Formats differ, and Harvard in particular varies between universities. Use your university library's referencing guide. [Purdue's library guide](https://guides.lib.purdue.edu/c.php?g=1371380&p=10135074) is useful for full APA examples and advice on acknowledging AI use.

## Keep evidence of your process

This is the habit most likely to protect you if a marker queries your work. Keep:

- **Shareable chat links** for every AI conversation related to the assignment
- **Drafts**: save new versions rather than overwriting one file
- **Version history**: Google Docs and Word online record it automatically, so don't delete it
- **Notes and plans**, including handwritten ones (photograph them)
- **Any emails** where a tutor confirmed what's allowed

If you're ever asked to explain your work, being able to show how it developed over time is very strong evidence.

## For sixth-formers: A-level and GCSE coursework (JCQ rules)

If you're doing NEA or other coursework, you're covered by the JCQ's [AI Use in Assessments](https://www.jcq.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2025/09/AI-Use-in-Assessments_Apr25_FINAL.pdf) guidance (revision two, 30 April 2025). Key points:

- **Failing to acknowledge AI tools you used as a source of information counts as misuse.** You also need to show *how* you used them.
- The 2025 update added real examples of candidate malpractice involving AI that wasn't properly referenced.
- Schools must make students aware of the consequences of AI misuse, which can include **disqualification**.
- Teachers must not use AI tools as the only way of marking your work.

[AQA's summary of the update](https://www.aqa.org.uk/blog/updated-jcq-guidance-use-of-artificial-intelligence-in-assessments) is a quick read. If you're unsure, ask your teacher before you start, not after you've submitted.

## Quick FAQ

**Can I use ChatGPT for my essay?**
It depends on the assessment. Check the brief, then the module handbook, then your department policy. If none of them clearly allows it, assume it isn't allowed and ask in writing.

**Do I have to declare AI if I only used it for brainstorming?**
Often yes. Many policies ask you to declare any use. Your assessment brief should say what form the declaration takes. If it doesn't, ask.

**Can I cite ChatGPT as a source for facts?**
It's better not to. Find the original source, check it and cite that. Cite the AI chat only for what the AI itself produced.

**My university gives us free AI tools. Does that mean I can use them in assessments?**
No. Oxford, for example, provides ChatGPT Edu but still requires explicit authorisation before AI is used in assessed work.

## Next steps

Before your next deadline, open the brief for each assignment and find the AI rule, or email your tutor if there isn't one. Turn on version history in whatever you write in, and get into the habit of saving shareable links to any AI chats you use. Save the declaration template above somewhere you can find it quickly, and bookmark your library's referencing guide for MLA or Harvard. Doing this now takes about 20 minutes and makes it far less likely you'll face an integrity query in December.
